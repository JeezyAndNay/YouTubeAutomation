Cappadocia sits in the high plateau of central Anatolia , in what is now Turkey, roughly equidistant between the Black Sea to the north and the Mediterranean to the south. It is not a place that looks important. The landscape is pale and surreal — volcanic tuff eroded over millennia into columns and cones the locals call fairy chimneys, rising in clusters across a terrain that seems carved by something other than weather. It is beautiful in the way that empty things are beautiful. It looks like a place where nothing is hidden.

That appearance is the oldest deception in the region. ... Cappadocia's geology is the key to everything that follows. Roughly three million years ago, two volcanoes — Mount Erciyes to the east and Hasan Dagi to the southwest — erupted repeatedly, blanketing the plateau in deep deposits of ash and pyroclastic material. That material compressed over time into volcanic tuff: a rock that is soft and workable when first exposed to air, then gradually hardens on contact with oxygen. Whoever first recognized what this stone could do understood they had been handed something rare. It could be carved with hand tools. It set like concrete once exposed. A tunnel cut into tuff is naturally insulated, maintains a constant temperature regardless of the season, and does not easily collapse.

People have lived in this landscape for a very long time. Hittites, Phrygians , Persians, Greeks, Romans, Byzantines — the plateau passed through every major power of the ancient world. For most of those groups, the underground was not merely a curiosity. It was a strategy. Written references to underground structures in Anatolia go back at least as far as 401 BC, when the Greek historian Xenophon , retreating through the region with his army, described houses dug into the earth — entrances above, living spaces below, wide enough for livestock to be brought inside. Xenophon was describing common domestic practice in this part of the world. ... Not an anomaly. A tradition.

By the time the Phrygians controlled this plateau, somewhere in the centuries between the 8th and 6th BC, that tradition had matured into something more deliberate. Rock-cut architecture was a Phrygian signature across Anatolia : cities carved into cliffsides, chambers linked by corridors, underground complexes built for purposes that combined storage, shelter, and sacred space. This is the context in which Derinkuyu sits — if you accept the mainstream attribution. And the attribution matters, because it means the structure did not appear in a vacuum. It was the culmination of a regional practice taken to its most extreme conclusion. What stands in the Derinkuyu district today is a small, quiet town. The streets are narrow. The buildings are stone. The surface offers nothing unusual to the eye. ... [dramatic tone] Under the streets, under the houses, under the basements of ordinary homes, something else entirely has been waiting for a very long time.

The year is 1963, and a resident of Derinkuyu is renovating his home. He is not an archaeologist. He is not looking for anything. He picks up a sledgehammer, walks to a basement wall that has always seemed oddly thick, and swings. The wall gives way. Not to stone or rubble. ... To air. Beyond the breach is a room that has no business being there — carved out of solid rock, its ceiling smooth, its floor worn. The air inside is cool in a way that has nothing to do with the season. On the far wall of the room is a corridor, cut at a slight angle, descending. He follows it.

The corridor opens onto another level. Then another. The geometry keeps repeating, expanding — not a cellar, not a root storage room, not any domestic space that makes sense in the context of a house above it. He is following something built on a scale he cannot immediately comprehend. What he has stumbled into — what he reports to Turkish authorities, triggering an archaeological survey that has now been running for decades — is a CITY. ... [awe] An entire urban complex dug straight into the volcanic rock, descending approximately 85 meters, roughly 280 feet, below the surface. Inverted, that is the height of a 28-story building.

The structure has, at minimum, up to 18 distinct levels, though the officially accessible count currently stands around 10 and the lower reaches remain only partially explored. The upper levels contain stables — carved stone troughs, channels for waste, spaces sized for animals brought underground during emergencies. There are storage rooms, communal kitchens, wine cellars, oil presses with grinding stones still in their channels. [dramatic tone] And then the ventilation system: approximately 52 vertical air shafts cut through all levels, the deepest reaching 55 meters, each PRECISELY positioned to create continuous airflow through the entire complex. The primary shaft also functioned as a well, tapping groundwater far below.

On the second level, archaeologists identify a large room with a barrel-vaulted ceiling and carved stone features consistent with a meeting hall or school — most commonly described as a missionary school. This is not a room where you survive. This is a room where you teach. On the deepest accessible level sits a cruciform church — a chapel carved in the shape of a cross, complete with a baptistery alcove. This is not a refugee camp. Whoever lived here brought their institutions underground with them. ... [pause] And then there are the doors.

At critical chokepoints throughout the tunnel system, massive circular stone discs are set into channels cut into the tunnel walls. Each one weighs somewhere between 200 and 500 kilograms. Each one is custom-fitted to its specific passage — not interchangeable, not approximate. They roll into place with minimal effort for their size, sealing the corridor completely. At the center of each door is a small hole, just large enough for a spear or lance to be inserted as a locking pin from the interior. [whispers] Every single one of these doors locks from the inside ONLY. ... Whatever was outside, the people in this city were not planning to open the door for it.

Mainstream archaeology's position on Derinkuyu is measured and largely consistent, even where it acknowledges the gaps. The soft volcanic tuff, scholars note, makes construction feasible in a way that surprises modern visitors but would have been straightforward with iron-age tooling. Fresh tuff can be worked with iron picks. It is not granite. It is not limestone. A disciplined workforce with the right tools could excavate these chambers over extended periods without requiring anything beyond the documented capabilities of the ancient world. The ventilation shafts — which appear as marvels to a modern eye — follow natural fault lines and existing geological channels in many cases, exploiting the terrain rather than overcoming it.

The most commonly accepted attribution for the original construction phases is Phrygian. Archaeologists point to the Phrygians' documented practice of rock-cut architecture throughout the region as the foundation for this claim. The explicitly Christian features of the deeper levels — the church, the baptistery, the missionary school — are attributed to the Byzantine period, when early Christian communities in Cappadocia adapted and expanded the structure during Roman persecution in the first through fourth centuries AD. The most heavily documented period of active use is the Arab-Byzantine wars of the 7th through 10th centuries. Byzantine Christian populations under repeated Arab raids used the underground cities of Cappadocia as their primary defensive strategy.

The millstone doors, in this reading, are sophisticated but explicable: custom stonework produced by skilled craftspeople serving a population that had both the need and the time to engineer precisely fitted defenses. The confirmed 9-kilometer tunnel connecting Derinkuyu to the neighboring underground city of Kaymakli is understood as a communications and logistics corridor linking refugee communities during sustained conflict. Capacity estimates — most commonly cited around 20,000 people, with some sources reaching as high as 50,000 when livestock and extended habitation are factored in — are accepted as consistent with a major regional refuge.

The honest version of the mainstream position: Derinkuyu is extraordinary but not inexplicable. Ancient people, facing ancient threats, used available materials with accumulated skill to build an underground refuge. The fact that it is large does not make it mysterious. The fact that it is sophisticated does not require an unnamed origin. That is the official story. It answers many of the questions. Here is the one it does not answer. There is no inscription anywhere in Derinkuyu. No founding text. No builder's mark. No dedication.

In a structure that contains a church, a school, and wine presses — in a culture that carved text into stone across the ancient world — the largest underground city in Turkey has no known author. The mainstream answer, "archaeologists believe it was the Phrygians," is itself an educated inference, not a documented attribution.

[curious] The absence of any founding inscription is not, by itself, evidence of anything beyond poor record-keeping. Ancient construction did not always come with documentation. But it opens a door — and beyond that door, the questions accumulate in ways the mainstream explanation struggles to fully absorb. ... Start with the ventilation system. Fifty-two air shafts. The deepest descends 55 meters. Each one precisely positioned across the full vertical extent of the complex to create functional airflow for thousands of people and their animals. For this system to work as designed, the builders had to plan the ENTIRE structure before excavation began. You cannot retrofit a functioning ventilation network into a tunnel system that already exists — the shafts have to be coordinated with the levels from the outset.

This is not a finding consistent with a refuge dug a little at a time over centuries by successive generations making local decisions. [dramatic tone] It implies a UNIFIED design. A blueprint. A comprehensive vision executed as a whole. ... Some researchers, including those associated with the alternative archaeology movement, argue this level of coordinated planning points to a civilization with capabilities the standard Phrygian attribution does not fully explain. The argument is not frivolous: top-down unified design of a structure this scale requires organizational and engineering knowledge that sits uncomfortably with what we know of Iron Age construction practice in the region. These claims are not accepted by mainstream archaeology, and that skepticism deserves weight. But the underlying question — why does the ventilation system imply unified design if the city grew organically over centuries — has not been satisfactorily resolved.

[awe] Then there is the question of what threat demanded this response. The millstone doors lock from the inside. Every one. The tunnels at key chokepoints are deliberately narrow — wide enough for a single person, creating natural kill zones where defenders could hold against larger forces indefinitely. The stables near the upper levels. The food stores centrally placed. The church and school at the bottom, in the parts of the complex farthest from the surface. This layout implies that whoever designed it understood survival would need to be sustained — not measured in days but in MONTHS or longer. ... You do not carve a barrel-vaulted teaching hall nearly 300 feet underground if you expect to go home in a few weeks.

Some researchers argue the permanence of this installation implies a threat that was not episodic but sustained — [whispers] something that made the surface not just dangerous but UNINHABITABLE for extended periods. The Arab raids of the 7th through 10th centuries are real and well-documented. But raids pass through. They do not last long enough to require a winery and a children's school in the basement. ... A smaller subset of alternative researchers moves further into speculation — invoking volcanic winters, climate events, or catastrophic geological disruptions that made the surface untenable. And at the outer fringe, theories invoking non-human construction or pre-diluvian civilizations circulate widely. To be direct about it: those theories have no physical supporting evidence at the site. They belong in a separate category from the engineering questions above.

[dramatic tone] What does not belong in that fringe category is this: less than HALF of Derinkuyu has been officially excavated. Turkish authorities have cited structural safety concerns for restricting access to the lower levels. No official statement has confirmed what exists at the bottom of the unmapped shafts — or confirmed that those shafts have a bottom that anyone has reached. ... The biggest underground city ever found in Turkey, and the bulk of it remains unopened.

[awe] Derinkuyu is not alone. That fact changes the entire frame of the investigation. Approximately 200 underground cities and tunnel complexes have been identified in Cappadocia. Most are smaller than Derinkuyu. Many remain only partially surveyed. They are distributed across the plateau, and at least some are linked by tunnels — the confirmed connection between Derinkuyu and Kaymakli runs 9 kilometers underground. Researchers believe additional connecting tunnels exist between other complexes in the region. The full extent of the Cappadocian subterranean system is not known. ... And the region keeps revealing new pieces entirely by accident.

In 2013, construction crews working on an urban renewal project in Nevşehir — the provincial capital directly adjacent to the Derinkuyu district — broke through into another underground city beneath the Byzantine citadel. Early assessments suggested the new complex could be LARGER than Derinkuyu. Excavation was ongoing as of the most recent available information, with full academic findings not yet published. The discovery made international headlines and then, as these things tend to do, faded before anyone publicly answered the obvious question: ... if a city potentially larger than Derinkuyu can sit undiscovered beneath a populated urban center until 2013, what else is down there?

[dramatic tone] This is the pattern worth holding onto. Derinkuyu was found because someone swung a sledgehammer at the wrong wall. The Nevşehir city was found because someone broke ground for a housing project. These are not the results of systematic archaeological survey. They are accidents. ... Which means the inventory of what exists underground in Cappadocia is not complete — it is an ongoing revelation, and the assumption that we understand the extent of this network is almost CERTAINLY wrong.

There is also the question of continuity. Hittites, Phrygians , early Christians, Byzantine Greeks — each successive culture that moved through this plateau found the underground infrastructure already waiting and chose to expand it rather than abandon it. That continuity of use across thousands of years and radically different civilizations suggests the underground was not merely useful. It was NECESSARY in a way that outlasted any single threat, any single culture, any single century.

The plateau above shows nothing. Walk across it and you see pale stone, fairy chimneys, the open horizon. Everything that was built to survive — everything that mattered enough to protect — is beneath your feet, still there, still largely unmapped, still being found.

Somewhere in the Derinkuyu district, there is still a house with a hole in its basement wall. The man who made that hole in 1963 was not looking for history. He was renovating his home. He swung his hammer, and the wall came down, and the darkness behind it was centuries deep — or millennia. No one knows. ... That is the honest answer, and it does not improve with time. Decades of excavation have confirmed what is in the city: the stables, the wine presses, the school, the church, the stone doors that only open from the inside. They have not answered the question that still sits at the bottom of the deepest unmapped shaft.

Who built this? What made tens of thousands of people — the exact number is still debated — choose to go underground and stay there long enough to need a classroom?
