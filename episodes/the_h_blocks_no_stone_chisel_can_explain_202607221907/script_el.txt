The researcher sets a caliper across a flat stone surface somewhere in Bolivia. The stone is gray andesite [AN-deh-site] — one of the hardest natural materials on earth, the kind of rock that will eat through a modern carbide grinding wheel in minutes. The surface stretches two meters. At altitude. At 12,500 feet above sea level, on a wind-scoured plateau where nothing should be this precise. The caliper closes. The reading comes back. ... [whispers] Under one millimeter of variance. Across two meters of hand-worked volcanic stone.

The researcher puts the tool down and looks at the block again. Then at the dozens of blocks around it — scattered across the plateau like furniture after a flood. Overturned. Displaced. Some of them 40 meters from where they should be, weighing more than 100 metric tons each. Someone built something here. The history books say they built it by hand, with copper tools, without wheels, without iron, at 12,500 feet above the sea. The measurements keep saying something else. ... And that gap — between what the official record says and what the stones refuse to stop telling us — is where this investigation begins.
